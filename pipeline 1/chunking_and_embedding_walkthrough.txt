CHUNKING + EMBEDDING WALKTHROUGH
Using a real example from Chapter One of the book
==================================================

GOAL
----
Before this stage, the book is just one giant block of text. A computer
cannot compare "meaning" of two pieces of text directly. So we:
  1. Cut the book into small pieces ("chunks") - chunking.py
  2. Turn each chunk into a list of numbers that represents its meaning
     ("embedding") - embedding.py
These numbers are what will later let us search "which part of the book
is relevant to this question?" by comparing number-lists instead of words.


STEP 1: load_book()
--------------------
Code:
    text = load_book(BOOK_PATH)

What it does: opens the .txt file and reads the WHOLE thing into one
long string. Nothing is split yet.

Real output:
    Type of 'text': <class 'str'>
    Total characters in whole book: 439742
    First 80 characters:
    "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone\n\n\nCHAPTER ONE\n\nTHE BOY WHO LIVED\n\nMr. and "

Note: \n is the "newline" character. \n\n (double newline) = a blank
line = a paragraph break in the original file. A single \n is just
where a sentence happened to wrap onto the next line in the .txt file,
not a real paragraph break.


STEP 2: split_into_chapters()
------------------------------
Code:
    pattern = re.compile(r"^CHAPTER [A-Z]+$", re.MULTILINE)
    headings = pattern.findall(text)
    pieces = pattern.split(text)[1:]

"pattern" is a regular expression (a search pattern for text).
r"^CHAPTER [A-Z]+$" means: "a line that starts with the word CHAPTER,
then a space, then one-or-more capital letters, then ends" - this
matches lines like "CHAPTER ONE" / "CHAPTER TWO", not sentences that
just happen to contain the word "chapter".

- findall() collects every matching heading -> found 17 headings
  ('CHAPTER ONE' ... 'CHAPTER SEVENTEEN').
- split() cuts the giant string apart AT every heading, throwing the
  heading text itself away, keeping everything in between as separate
  pieces. [1:] drops the very first piece (the book title, which comes
  before "CHAPTER ONE" and isn't a chapter).

Real output:
    Number of (heading, body) pairs found: 17
    chapters[0] heading: 'CHAPTER ONE'
    chapters[0] body (first 200 chars):
    "THE BOY WHO LIVED\n\nMr. and Mrs. Dursley, of number four, Privet Drive,
    were proud to say\n..."

So chapters[0] is a pair: ('CHAPTER ONE', 'THE BOY WHO LIVED\n\nMr. and
Mrs...'). The heading was cleanly extracted; the body still has its raw
\n and \n\n characters, untouched.


STEP 3: split_into_paragraphs()
---------------------------------
Code:
    raw_paragraphs = chapter_text.split("\n\n")
    ...
    joined = " ".join(raw.split("\n"))

First line: cuts the chapter body wherever there's a blank line (\n\n)
- that's how we detect "this is a new paragraph." Chapter One split
into 109 pieces this way (including the title line as piece 0).

Second line is the cleanup step: take one paragraph, split it on single
\n (the mid-sentence line wraps), then glue the pieces back together
with a plain space instead of a newline.

Real BEFORE (raw from file - notice the \n in the middle of sentences):
"Mr. and Mrs. Dursley, of number four, Privet Drive, were proud to say\nthat
they were perfectly normal, thank you very much. They were the last\npeople
you'd expect to be involved in anything strange or mysterious,\nbecause they
just didn't hold with such nonsense."

Real AFTER (paragraphs[1] - one continuous line now):
"Mr. and Mrs. Dursley, of number four, Privet Drive, were proud to say that
they were perfectly normal, thank you very much. They were the last people
you'd expect to be involved in anything strange or mysterious, because they
just didn't hold with such nonsense."

Same words, but now reads as one continuous block instead of four choppy
lines. This matters because the embedding model reads text as natural
language - leftover mid-sentence line breaks would look like noise to it.


CLARIFICATION: what \n and \n\n actually mean (tiny example)
-----------------------------------------------------------
If this content in Notepad looked like:

    The cat sat.
    It was happy.

    The dog ran fast.

That's 3 visible lines of text plus one blank line. But Python does NOT
see "lines" the way your eyes do - it sees ONE long string of
characters, and every time you hit Enter in the file, Python records it
as an invisible character called \n. So that Notepad content, to
Python, is really just this one string:

    'The cat sat.\nIt was happy.\n\nThe dog ran fast.'

Read it character by character: "...sat." then \n (Enter was pressed
once) then "It was happy." then \n\n (Enter was pressed TWICE IN A ROW
- that's what makes a blank line) then "The dog ran fast."

RULE: one \n = one Enter press. Two \n in a row = a blank line = "new
paragraph."

Step A: mini_text.split("\n\n") -> cuts ONLY where there were two \n in
a row (the blank line):
    ['The cat sat.\nIt was happy.', 'The dog ran fast.']
2 pieces. Notice pieces[0] STILL has a \n inside it - that's because
there was only one Enter press between "sat." and "It", not a blank
line, so split("\n\n") left it alone. This piece is our ONE paragraph,
but it's still messy.

Step B: pieces[0].split("\n") -> now break that ONE piece apart by
single Enters:
    ['The cat sat.', 'It was happy.']
A list of 2 separate sentence-lines.

Step C: " ".join(lines) -> glue them back with a SPACE instead of a
newline:
    'The cat sat. It was happy.'
One clean sentence, no leftover \n characters.

SUMMARY - the entire trick, done in 2 cuts:
  1. Cut on \n\n first -> gives you the paragraphs (each paragraph
     might still have single \n's inside it).
  2. For each paragraph, cut on \n and glue back with spaces -> removes
     those leftover single \n's, leaving clean readable text.

So: many lines -> one paragraph (when there's no blank line between
them). One line does NOT equal one paragraph. A paragraph is defined by
the BLANK line around it, not by where the text happens to wrap.


STEP 4: chunk_chapter()
-------------------------
This is the part with the most moving pieces. For this demo we
temporarily used max_tokens=60 (instead of the real production value of
400) so that a "chunk closes and a new one starts" would actually
happen within just 4 paragraphs, instead of needing dozens of
paragraphs to hit 400.

The 4 real paragraphs going in:
    paragraph[0]: 45 words, 61 tokens   "Mr. and Mrs. Dursley, of number four..."
    paragraph[1]: 85 words, 109 tokens  "Mr. Dursley was the director of a firm..."
    paragraph[2]: 131 words, 183 tokens "The Dursleys had everything they wanted..."
    paragraph[3]: 67 words, 83 tokens   "When Mr. and Mrs. Dursley woke up..."

IMPORTANT: tokens are NOT the same as words. Paragraph 0 has 45 words
but 61 tokens - the tokenizer (the piece that turns text into the
numbered units the model actually reads) sometimes splits ONE word into
MULTIPLE tokens (e.g. "didn't" might become "didn" + "'" + "t").

Tracing the loop by hand against the code:

Paragraph 0: current_words starts empty, so the "close the chunk" check
is skipped (nothing to close yet). Paragraph 0 gets added:
current_words = its 45 words, current_tokens = 61.

Paragraph 1: Check: current_tokens (61) + paragraph_tokens (109) = 170,
which is > max_tokens (60). So -> CLOSE THE CHUNK NOW, before adding
paragraph 1:
    chunk[0]: 45 words, 61 tokens
    "Mr. and Mrs. Dursley... because they just didn't hold with such nonsense."
(That's exactly paragraph 0, saved as-is.)

Then the OVERLAP kicks in:
    current_words = current_words[-overlap_words:]
grabs the LAST 10 words of the chunk we just closed (overlap_words=10
in this demo) - "...or mysterious, because they just didn't hold with
such nonsense." - and paragraph 1 gets appended after it. chunk[1]'s
actual text literally STARTS by repeating the tail of chunk[0]:
    chunk[1]: 95 words, 123 tokens
    "or mysterious, because they just didn't hold with such nonsense. Mr.
    Dursley was the director of a firm called Grunnings..."

That repeated tail is the overlap - it exists so that if a search later
lands near this seam, it doesn't lose context that got cut mid-thought.

WHY is chunk[1] already at 123 tokens, way over our 60 limit? Because
paragraph 1 ALONE is 109 tokens - bigger than max_tokens=60. The code's
rule is "never split a paragraph apart," so once a paragraph itself is
close to (or here, bigger than) the limit, the chunk holding it WILL
overflow. This is not a bug - it's the exact tradeoff documented in the
code's own docstring ("this assumes no single paragraph is longer than
max_tokens"). This is exactly why the REAL production code uses
max_tokens=400 - we verified earlier that the book's longest paragraph
is only 199 words (roughly ~260-280 tokens), safely under 400. With
max_tokens=60 in this demo, that assumption breaks, and we see the
failure mode live.

The same pattern repeats for chunk[2] and chunk[3] - each new chunk
opens with the last 10 words of the previous one, then absorbs the next
paragraph whole.


STEP 5: embedding one real chunk
-----------------------------------
Code:
    vector = model.encode(one_chunk_text)

Took chunk[0]'s actual text (the "Dursleys proud to say" paragraph) and
embedded it. Real output:

    Shape: (384,)  -> a flat list of 384 numbers
    First 10:  [0.0304  0.0016  0.0709  0.0236  0.0202  0.0053  0.0268 -0.0224  0.0013 -0.0648]
    Last 10:   [0.1248 -0.0727  0.0472  0.1139 -0.0628  0.0419  0.0863  0.0343 -0.1080  0.0210]

That entire 268-character paragraph about the Dursleys got compressed
into exactly 384 decimal numbers. Every chunk, short or long, always
produces 384 numbers - that count is fixed by the model itself, not by
how long the input text is.


STEP 6: proof these numbers actually capture MEANING
--------------------------------------------------------
To prove these numbers aren't arbitrary, we embedded 3 made-up
sentences and measured how "close" their vectors are using cosine
similarity (1.0 = identical meaning, 0 = unrelated, closer to 1 = more
similar):

    A: "Harry Potter lived with the Dursleys."
    B: "Harry stayed at his aunt and uncle's house."  (different words, same meaning as A)
    C: "Quidditch is played on broomsticks."          (unrelated topic)

    Similarity(A, B) = 0.6446   <- higher, despite sharing almost no words
    Similarity(A, C) = 0.2941   <- lower

A and B share barely any words in common, but scored much closer
together than A and C - because the model encoded MEANING, not just
matching words. That's the whole point of embeddings, and this is what
will let retrieval later find the right book chunk even if a question
uses totally different words than the book does.


STEP 7: what a vector database is for
--------------------------------------
So far, every embedding only existed in memory - the moment the Python
script finished running, all 370 vectors disappeared. A vector database
(we're using chromadb, already installed) solves two problems:
  1. Saves the vectors to disk, so we don't have to re-embed all 370
     chunks every single time we want to use them.
  2. Later lets us quickly find "which stored vectors are closest to
     this new vector?" - which is exactly what searching for an answer
     to a question will require.

Three building blocks, with a real-world analogy:
  - CLIENT: the connection/app that talks to the on-disk database
    folder. Analogy: the Gmail app vs. Google's raw mail storage - the
    client is how you reach the storage, not the storage itself.
  - COLLECTION: a named "drawer" inside that database (we named ours
    "sorcerers_stone_chunks"). One database can hold several collections.
  - RECORD: one stored chunk = 4 aligned pieces bundled together under
    one id: the id itself, the embedding (vector), the document
    (original chunk text), and metadata (extra tags, e.g. which
    chapter). The vector alone is useless to a human, so the database
    stores the original text right alongside it.


STEP 8: creating a client and an empty collection (real demo)
-----------------------------------------------------------------
Code:
    client = chromadb.PersistentClient(path="chroma_demo")
    collection = client.create_collection(name="test_collection")
    print(collection)
    print(collection.count())

Real output:
    Collection(name=test_collection)
    0

Before this ran, the "chroma_demo" folder didn't exist. After one line
of code, it existed on disk with a chroma.sqlite3 file inside - proof
the client is a real connection to real persistent storage, not just an
in-memory object. count() returns 0 because we made an empty drawer;
nothing has been put inside it yet.


STEP 9: adding one record by hand (real demo)
--------------------------------------------------
Code:
    collection.add(
        ids=["chunk_0"],
        embeddings=[[0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4]],   # shrunk to 4 numbers just for this demo
        documents=["Harry looked up and saw a snowy owl tapping at the window."],
        metadatas=[{"chapter": "Chapter 1"}],
    )
    result = collection.get(ids=["chunk_0"])

Real output:
    Count after adding: 1
    {'ids': ['chunk_0'],
     'embeddings': None,
     'documents': ['Harry looked up and saw a snowy owl tapping at the window.'],
     'metadatas': [{'chapter': 'Chapter 1'}]}

Adding a record is really just "here are 4 lists (ids, embeddings,
documents, metadatas), all the same length, lined up by position." The
count going from 0 to 1 confirms the record actually landed. Note
'embeddings': None here - not missing, just not returned by default
(nobody wants to read a huge list of decimals); passing
include=["embeddings"] to get() would show it.


STEP 10: storing all 370 real chunks (storage.py)
-------------------------------------------------------
Code (the real, non-demo version, in storage.py):
    client = chromadb.PersistentClient(path="chroma_db")
    collection = client.get_or_create_collection(
        name="sorcerers_stone_chunks",
        metadata={"hnsw:space": "cosine"},
    )
    collection.add(
        ids=[f"chunk_{i}" for i in range(len(chunks))],
        embeddings=embeddings,       # all 370 real 384-dim vectors
        documents=texts,             # all 370 real chunk texts
        metadatas=[{"chapter": c["chapter"]} for c in chunks],
    )

New details vs. the one-record demo:
  - ids/metadatas are now built automatically with a list comprehension
    (chunk_0 ... chunk_369) instead of typed by hand.
  - get_or_create_collection (not create_collection) so re-running the
    script later doesn't crash if the collection already exists.
  - metadata={"hnsw:space": "cosine"} tells chromadb to measure
    similarity using COSINE SIMILARITY specifically, matching how the
    embedding model (multi-qa-MiniLM-L6-cos-v1) was trained. Without
    this, chromadb defaults to a different distance formula that lines
    up less well with this model.
  - The database now lives in "chroma_db" inside the real project
    folder (not a scratchpad), so it survives between sessions.

Real output:
    Built 370 chunks.
    Generating embeddings for all chunks...
    Adding 370 records to collection 'sorcerers_stone_chunks'...
    Done. Collection now holds 370 records.

    Sample record back from the database (chunk_5):
      Chapter: CHAPTER ONE
      Text: saying. "The Potters, that's right, that's what I heard
      yes, their son, Harry" Mr. Dursley stopped dead...

All 370 chunks - the entire book - are now permanently saved on disk,
each as a complete, readable, searchable record.


STEP 11: tracing ONE chunk through the ENTIRE pipeline, start to finish
------------------------------------------------------------------------
To double check nothing got scrambled anywhere along the way, we
followed chunk_0 (the very first chunk of the book) through every
single stage and confirmed each handoff:

  1. Raw text -> chapters: found heading 'CHAPTER ONE'.
  2. Chapter -> paragraphs: Chapter One has 109 paragraphs (paragraph 0
     is the title, skipped).
  3. Paragraphs -> chunk: paragraphs 1..N glued together into
     chunk_0 = 261 words, 353 tokens (under the real 400-token limit).
  4. Chunk text -> embedding: turned into a vector of 384 numbers,
     e.g. first 5: [-0.0335  0.0243  0.0604  0.0299  -0.0209].
  5. Read the SAME chunk back out of the real ChromaDB database:
       Stored chapter tag: CHAPTER ONE
       Stored text matches freshly-chunked text: True
       Cosine similarity between fresh vector and stored vector: 1.000000

Text match = True and similarity = 1.000000 together prove the record
sitting in the database is exactly the same chunk and exactly the same
vector we computed - nothing was corrupted, truncated, or mismatched
between embedding and storage.

Full chain for this one example:
    book file
      -> "CHAPTER ONE" heading found
      -> 109 paragraphs
      -> paragraphs 1..N glued into a 353-token chunk
      -> 384-number vector
      -> stored on disk with id "chunk_0", tagged CHAPTER ONE
